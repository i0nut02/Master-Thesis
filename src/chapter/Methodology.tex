\chapter{Experimental Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter describes how the two host-CPU configurations, the NVIDIA A100
system, and the Tenstorrent implementation are compared in terms of
throughput, search quality, and energy. Because the Tenstorrent implementation
described in Chapter~\ref{chap:design-implementation} does not associate
\(N_{\mathrm{probe}}\) with the same candidate set as conventional per-query IVF
search, cross-platform comparisons are made at matched measured recall rather
than at matched index parameters.

\section{Evaluation Goals}
\label{sec:evaluation-goals}

The experiments address the following evaluation goals:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item compare throughput as a function of measured Recall@10 rather than at
    one isolated search setting;
    \item determine how \(N_{\mathrm{list}}\) and \(N_{\mathrm{probe}}\)
    affect the trade-off between throughput and recall;
    \item identify the dominant portions of the Tenstorrent search path and
    inspect overlap and imbalance within its fine-search program;
    \item compare the energy of a complete query workload at approximately
    matched recall.
\end{enumerate}

A higher-throughput operating point at lower recall is never treated as
preferable to a slower point at higher recall unless the quality difference is
made explicit.

combines NVIDIA A100-SXM 64 GB accelerators, with a power limit of

8358 host in two NUMA domains. The CPU and GPU baselines used the same
Python~3.11.7 environment with Faiss~1.14.1 and GCC~12.2.0, and the GPU runs
additionally used CUDA~12.2 and NVIDIA driver 535.274.02. The Tenstorrent host

work. The A100 variant is stated explicitly because PCIe and SXM models differ
in memory and power characteristics \cite{nvidia_a100_spec}; the Wormhole n150
follows Tenstorrent's board specifications \cite{tenstorrent_wormhole_spec}.

\section{Dataset and Index Parameters}
\label{sec:dataset-index-parameters}

All systems search the ANN-Benchmarks \texttt{glove-100-angular} dataset
\cite{ann_benchmarks,ann_benchmarks_datasets}, built from the 100-dimensional
Stanford GloVe Twitter vectors \cite{pennington_glove} with a fixed train/test
split. It contains 1,183,514 database vectors and 10,000 test queries, together
with the 100 exact nearest neighbors of every query under angular distance.
Database vectors, centroids, and queries are normalized so that angular
similarity reduces to an inner product.

For each \(N_{\mathrm{list}}\), centroids are trained once with Faiss
\(k\)-means on the training split and saved to file. Faiss's default
settings are used, which sample at most 256 training vectors per centroid. Every implementation loads
the same centroid matrix and assigns the database vectors to inverted lists
itself, so clustering quality is not an experimental variable. On Tenstorrent,
vectors, queries, centroids, and score tiles use FP16, identifiers use
\texttt{UInt32}, and FP32 destination accumulation is enabled because the
top-\(k\) path requires it (Section~\ref{subsec:tiled-list-layout}). The
compute kernels use the default TT-Metalium math fidelity, HiFi4. Because
the multiplier of the Tensix matrix engine is narrower than an FP16 mantissa,
each product is computed in one to four passes over parts of the mantissas:
the lower fidelities, LoFi to HiFi3, use fewer passes and drop the least
significant bits to run faster, whereas HiFi4 uses all four and multiplies the
complete mantissas. The similarity scores are therefore computed at the full
precision of the FP16 inputs, which matters in nearest-neighbor search, where
close candidates can differ only in the last bits of their scores.

All four configurations are evaluated on the grid
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{list}}\in\{512,1024,2048\},
\qquad
N_{\mathrm{probe}}\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}.
\label{eq:parameter-grid}
\end{equation}
The Faiss CPU and GPU runs add \((N_{\mathrm{list}},N_{\mathrm{probe}})=(512,64)\)
to reach the recall of Tenstorrent at \((512,32)\), and Tenstorrent adds
\((2048,12)\) as a recall-matching point for the energy comparison. Because the
Tenstorrent implementation maintains top-32 states, it supports
\(N_{\mathrm{probe}}\leq32\) and \(k\leq32\). All quality results use
\(k=10\), matching the public ANN-Benchmarks configuration for this dataset
\cite{ann_benchmarks,ann_benchmarks_datasets}.

Changing \(N_{\mathrm{list}}\) affects both IVF stages: more lists increase the
coarse centroid scan and reduce the mean inverted-list size
\begin{equation}
\overline{L}=\frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{list}}},
\label{eq:mean-list-size}
\end{equation}
which falls by a factor of four from \(N_{\mathrm{list}}=512\) to 2048, although
the work for a given query still depends on the non-uniform sizes of the lists
actually selected.

For Faiss, \(N_{\mathrm{probe}}\) is the number of lists searched for each
query. Tenstorrent instead searches, for every query in a batch of up to 32, the
union of the lists selected by all queries in that batch
(Equation~\ref{eq:batch-union}). A query can therefore be scored against lists
selected by other queries, so equal \(N_{\mathrm{probe}}\) values do not imply
equal candidate sets or recall.

\section{Recall, Throughput, and Matching}
\label{sec:evaluation-metrics}

Recall@\(k\) follows Equation~\ref{eq:recall-at-k} and is reported as the mean
over the 10,000 queries; throughout the evaluation, ``recall'' means Recall@10.
Recall is computed in separate untimed quality runs, so ground-truth
comparison never inflates search time or energy.

The principal performance metric is queries per second (QPS). If repetition
\(r\) of \(Q_{\mathrm{rep}}=10{,}000\) queries takes \(t_r\), then
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{QPS}_r=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{rep}}}{t_r},
\qquad
\operatorname{QPS}_{\mathrm{reported}}
=\operatorname{median}_{r=1,\ldots,10}\left(\operatorname{QPS}_r\right).
\label{eq:median-qps}
\end{equation}

CPU and GPU results can be compared at common
\((N_{\mathrm{list}},N_{\mathrm{probe}})\), whereas comparisons involving
Tenstorrent use measured recall. The energy comparison uses four operating
points, grouped around nominal targets of 60\%, 78\%, 88\%, and 95\%
(Table~\ref{tab:recall-matched-points}); these labels denote approximate
quality matching, not uncertainty intervals. The Faiss configurations are
shared by the CPU and GPU. Because both use the same centroids and the same
per-query list selection, their recall is almost identical: it differs by at
most 0.01 percentage points at these four points and by at most 0.04 across
the whole grid, that is, by at most about 10 and 40 of the 100,000 true
neighbors of the 10,000 queries. The remaining difference arises because the
two implementations compute distances with a different order of
floating-point operations and select the best candidates with different
algorithms, so near-ties between centroids or candidates are occasionally
resolved differently. The
largest mismatch is at the 60\% target, where Tenstorrent is 1.78 percentage
points above Faiss, because it was the closest Tenstorrent configuration available.

\section{Benchmark Procedure}
\label{sec:benchmark-procedure}

\paragraph{Throughput runs.}
Each configuration executes ten timed repetitions of 10,000 queries within one
process. For Faiss, the index is initialized once and the ten
\texttt{index.search} calls reuse the same index, query matrix, and, on the GPU, the same
CUDA context; each call receives all 10,000 queries at once. The Faiss runs use no dedicated warm-up call, but the per-run
values show no systematic first-repetition slowdown, and the median is
insensitive to a single outlier. The Tenstorrent stage and device-profiler
experiments include a preceding warm-up invocation, which is excluded from the
reported medians and trace. Search timing excludes index construction and the
one-time index upload, but includes query-dependent preparation, execution,
synchronization, and final result transfer
(Section~\ref{sec:tt-stage-method}).

On Tenstorrent, each 10,000-query repetition is split into
\begin{equation}
B_{10k}=\left\lceil\frac{10{,}000}{32}\right\rceil=313
\label{eq:tt-batches-per-repetition}
\end{equation}
internal batches of at most 32 queries; the last batch holds 16 valid queries
and 16 padded rows, which generate no fine-search tasks. Queries keep their
dataset order and are grouped consecutively without shuffling, so the batch
unions are reproducible; sensitivity to other groupings is not evaluated.

Run-to-run stability is checked with the relative standard deviation
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{RSD}_{\mathrm{QPS}}
=100\,\frac{s_{\mathrm{QPS}}}{\overline{\operatorname{QPS}}},
\label{eq:qps-rsd}
\end{equation}
where \(s_{\mathrm{QPS}}\) and \(\overline{\operatorname{QPS}}\) are the sample
standard deviation and mean of the ten repetitions.

\paragraph{Energy sessions.}
Energy is measured on a complete workload rather than on the search calls
alone. One session starts a new process, loads the dataset and the saved
centroids, assigns the database vectors to inverted lists, transfers the index
to the accelerator where applicable, and then executes ten repetitions of
10,000 queries,
\begin{equation}
Q_{\mathrm{run}}=10\times10{,}000=100{,}000
\label{eq:queries-per-run}
\end{equation}
searches in total, before terminating. \(k\)-means training and recall
evaluation are excluded. Each operating point is measured in ten independent
sessions, and the reported energy is the median across them. The
spread across sessions is reported as the interquartile range and as the RSD,
defined as in Equation~\ref{eq:qps-rsd} with session energy in place of QPS.
Session time is likewise the median over the ten sessions, and average power
is the median energy divided by the median session time. On the CPU node and
on the Tenstorrent host, the RAPL counters of both sockets are summed.

This workload boundary deliberately charges initialization to the query
workload: it represents the cost of answering 100,000 queries starting from a
cold process. Platforms whose search is fast relative to their initialization
therefore spend most of the session outside the search calls.
Section~\ref{sec:energy-results} reports this share explicitly.

\section{Tenstorrent Stage and Device Profiling}
\label{sec:tt-stage-method}

The Tenstorrent search path is partitioned into six disjoint host-observed
intervals, all measured with \texttt{std::chrono::steady\_clock} over ten
repetitions of 10,000 queries after one warm-up invocation, and reported as
per-stage medians:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T_{\mathrm{search}}={}&T_{\mathrm{H2D}}
+T_{\mathrm{CPU,coarse}}
+T_{\mathrm{TT,coarse}}\\
&+T_{\mathrm{CPU,fine}}
+T_{\mathrm{TT,fine}}
+T_{\mathrm{CPU,output}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tt-stage-sum-method}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \(T_{\mathrm{H2D}}\): blocking transfer of query and fine-worker
    configuration data to the device, excluding the one-time index upload.
    \item \(T_{\mathrm{CPU,coarse}}\): host query preparation, buffer and
    program setup, and coarse-search runtime configuration.
    \item \(T_{\mathrm{TT,coarse}}\): coarse-workload enqueue through
    \texttt{distributed::Finish()}.
    \item \(T_{\mathrm{CPU,fine}}\): coarse-result handling, batch-level
    list-union construction, worker scheduling, and fine-program configuration.
    \item \(T_{\mathrm{TT,fine}}\): fine-workload enqueue through completion,
    including worker execution and final device-side sorting.
    \item \(T_{\mathrm{CPU,output}}\): final device-to-host transfers,
    untilization, and construction of the returned scores and identifiers.
\end{itemize}

The two TT intervals include device compute, DRAM and NoC traffic, and
synchronization, so they describe complete accelerator stages rather than
isolated compute or memory costs.

To relate fine-search cost to the data scanned, a separate untimed run logs,
for every internal batch and each of the 19 Tenstorrent configurations, the
lists in the batch union \(\mathcal{U}_b\), their pages, and the pages assigned
to each of the 63 workers. It uses the lists selected by the device coarse
search, and one repetition of 10,000 queries suffices because the batching is
deterministic; a repeated run at \((512,8)\) produced identical unions. From
this log, the scanned fraction is the number of vectors in the union divided
by the 1,183,514 database vectors, and it is compared with the vectors in the
query's own \(N_{\mathrm{probe}}\) lists, which is what Faiss scans. Worker
imbalance is the largest per-worker page load of a batch divided by the mean
over all 63 workers. The log treats every list as a single task, whereas the
timed runs split lists longer than 128 pages into chunks, which occurs only
with 512 lists
(Section~\ref{subsec:greedy-work-distribution}), so its imbalance values are
upper bounds for the timed runs.

Two device-profiler captures, both taken after the warm-up, examine the fine
search. The profiler reports device coordinates: profiler core \((1,1)\) is
program-logical core \((0,0)\), reserved for final aggregation, and profiler
cores \((1,2)\) to \((1,4)\) are three consecutive fine-search workers. The
first capture, at \((N_{\mathrm{list}},N_{\mathrm{probe}})=(512,8)\), shows
the last three batches of the measured invocation, with every RISC processor
of these four cores on a separate row; each worker zone covers one list task. The second, at \((512,1)\), zooms into 16 consecutive
pages of a single list on one worker, with reader zones for the query fetch
and each page's DRAM fetch, and compute zones for each page. Cycle counts are
shifted to a common origin,
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{relative}}=C-C_0,
\label{eq:relative-device-cycles}
\end{equation}
where \(C_0\) is the earliest displayed timestamp, and converted to
microseconds at the device frequency of about 1~GHz.
Because reader, compute, and writer kernels communicate through circular
buffers, their zones can include backpressure, input waits, and semaphore
synchronization. The trace is therefore used to study overlap and per-core
imbalance, not to estimate raw DRAM latency or bandwidth.

\section{Power and Energy Measurement}
\label{sec:power-energy-method}

CPU energy is read from cumulative RAPL counters. For the accelerator systems,
device power is sampled through \texttt{nvidia-smi} or \texttt{tt-smi} and
host energy through RAPL; the two contributions are integrated separately and
added. Although power is sampled rather than measured continuously, the
sampling is not expected to introduce a significant error. RAPL counters are
cumulative, so the energy between two readings does not depend on the
sampling interval, and each device trace collects enough samples to follow
the power profile: about 20--40 per session for the A100, which is read
roughly every 0.13~s, and about 19--35 for the Wormhole, which is read roughly
every 0.73~s. Since power stays nearly constant within each phase of a
session, such as idle, initialization, and search, the trapezoidal
integration tracks it closely, and taking the median over ten sessions
reduces whatever sampling error remains.

On every host, RAPL energy is the sum of one top-level package counter
(\texttt{package-*}) per socket. Their sub-domains, such as the core
domain, are not added, so no energy is counted twice. The package domain
covers the processor chip, including its cores, caches, and memory controller,
but not the DRAM modules, power supply, fans, or storage, so the host values
are a lower bound on the energy of the whole node. Although the Intel hosts
also expose a separate RAPL domain for the DRAM modules, it is deliberately
left out so that the same package-only boundary is used on all three
systems. The AMD EPYC host of the Tenstorrent system provides no DRAM domain.
It lists one package domain per die, eight in total over its two sockets, but
the four domains of a socket report the same socket-wide counter, so its host
energy is read from one domain per socket to avoid counting it four times.

\paragraph{CPU.}
The Slurm job enables energy profiling, and a background sampler reads the
cumulative \texttt{energy\_uj} counters exposed through
\texttt{/sys/class/powercap} at a nominal interval of 0.1~s. For an aggregate
cumulative reading \(R_{\mathrm{RAPL}}(t)\) in microjoules,
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{CPU}}
=10^{-6}\left(R_{\mathrm{RAPL}}(t_{\mathrm{end}})
-R_{\mathrm{RAPL}}(t_{\mathrm{start}})\right).
\label{eq:workload-energy}
\end{equation}
Slurm affinity and the number of cores visible to Faiss were checked for each
allocation.

\paragraph{GPU.}
One background stream samples the A100 board power from \texttt{nvidia-smi}
and another samples the host's cumulative RAPL energy, with requested sleeps of
50~ms and 100~ms. Command execution makes the effective cadence slower, so the
recorded timestamps, not the requested periods, are used. The GPU trace is
integrated with Equation~\ref{eq:tt-host-energy} and
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{GPU+host}}=E_{\mathrm{GPU}}+E_{\mathrm{CPU,host}}.
\label{eq:gpu-host-energy}
\end{equation}
For one session at the \((512,64)\) operating point, the board trace
\(E_{\mathrm{GPU}}\) is also reported on its own, to compare the two
accelerators device to device.

\paragraph{Tenstorrent.}
A Python profiler launches the C++ benchmark, polls device power through
\texttt{tt-smi}, and records the host's cumulative RAPL counter. The external
\texttt{tt-smi} call dominates the polling cadence: for the inspected
\((512,32)\) run, the median gap between samples is about 0.73~s. \texttt{tt-smi}
reports the chip power telemetry of the Wormhole firmware \cite{tt_smi},
whereas \texttt{nvidia-smi} reports the power of the entire A100 board
\cite{nvidia_smi}, so the two device boundaries are not identical.

\paragraph{Integration.}
For a sampled power trace with samples \((t_i,P_i)\), \(i=1,\ldots,m\), energy is
estimated with the trapezoidal rule on the recorded timestamps,
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{trace}}
=\sum_{i=2}^{m}\frac{P_{i-1}+P_i}{2}\left(t_i-t_{i-1}\right),
\label{eq:tt-host-energy}
\end{equation}
while cumulative RAPL counters use the difference in
Equation~\ref{eq:workload-energy}. Energy is never reconstructed from an
unweighted mean of power samples. Average power, where reported, is derived as
integrated energy divided by elapsed session time. Amortized energy per query
is
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{query}}=\frac{E_{\mathrm{run}}}{100{,}000},
\label{eq:energy-per-query}
\end{equation}
where \(E_{\mathrm{run}}\) is the median session energy; it includes the
initialization share of the session and is not the energy of an isolated query.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:methodology-summary}

Throughput is the median of ten 10,000-query search repetitions, recall is
evaluated outside all timed and monitored intervals, and energy is the median
over ten complete 100,000-query sessions that include initialization. Because
the Tenstorrent batch-level union changes the candidate set, cross-platform
comparisons are matched by measured Recall@10. The next chapter reports the
resulting throughput, stage decomposition, and energy.
